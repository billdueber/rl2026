Eleven Years and Change

Eleven Years…​

It’s been more than 11 years since I wrote a blog post.

That seems impossibly long ago now. The kids were 7, 5, and 2, and just trying to get to the end of the day felt like a struggle a lot of the time. I was going through a mid-life crisis — no red sports car, which I don’t know what I would have done with, anyway, but a lot of self-doubt and anxiety about whether I had completely lost any agency in my own life.

Young kids can do that to you, I guess.

My day-to-day work life was built around trying to improve the library catalog results and, even more so, trying to prove to myself (and thus hopefully others) that the stuff I was producing was, you know, good. Impressive, even.

Note

Writing is the act of trying to convince yourself that you know what you’re talking about.

I saw blogging as a mechanism to force myself to write, building on an ingrained belief that writing is a critical component of thinking in general — that ideas, when expressed in print, emerge more fully formed and solid than beforehand. Writing, I’ve tried to convey to my kids, is the act of trying to convince yourself that you know what you’re talking about. If you haven’t written it down, you haven’t really thought it through.

Then Google Reader went dark, and I stopped writing. Apparently, all my lip service to critical thinking and high-minded exploration of ideas was completely subsumed by ego and the desire to be read.

I don’t feel too bad about it. A lot of us stopped blogging around the same time. Dancing like no one is watching is one thing, but writing when no one is reading is clearly another. I’m married to a writer, so I have a little insight into this.

I didn’t totally abandon the blog, of course. I just stopped writing in it. I changed the technology it was based on more than once, in lieu of actually writing, and managed to lose the comments and a lot of the formatting and such along the way. None of the old stuff is particularly interesting, anyway, and the posts with a more technical lean are so hilariously out of date that they’re more "fun" than useful.

I’ve been having a pretty good time going over the old posts. Part of it is the glimpses of a Me-Back-Then, who was living a remarkably similar life to the one I’m living now, especially my work life. Still working at the University of Michigan Libraries, still focused on programming and trying to make search better. Many of the technologies have changed, as have the types of data I’m dealing with on the regular, but it’s nothing I wouldn’t have recognized ten years ago. Libraries aren’t particularly dynamic, no matter what we tell ourselves.

The tone on those old posts is a little…​strident, maybe? I had clearly heard someone use the phrase, "Personal Brand" at some point and taken it to heart. But it’s mostly good or good-enough work, and I’m still the best audience for my own humor, so that’s all a lot of fun.

A big part of what happened over the last ten years is that, in a lot of ways, I "settled into" the job. I acquired all that vaunted "Organizational knowledge" that I was so quick to mock in the past. I got more familiar with the data we deal with, and the methods we use. I got to know more people at more institutions, and then watch most of them move on as they got older, switching careers or moving into management. I like to think that I didn’t get complacent, but I did get, for lack of a better word, "comfortable."

My hair got gray, my forehead turned into a "five-head" ("Like a forehead, but One Bigger"), and some recent retirements turned me into the Old Man of my group. Given that I have a 14-year-old kid, retirement is not going to be an option anytime soon, so I’ll be soldiering on despite being 57 now. Things haven’t always been smooth, but they were always at least a little familiar.

…​and Change

I didn’t fool myself into thinking things were static, of course. My technical expertise fell out of date, colleagues started getting younger and smarter, all of which made me think fondly (and/or bitterly, depending on the day) of when I was able to consider myself Young and Smart.

Code4Lib turned into a conference that was more welcoming to more interests and skill levels, which is an unambiguously Good Thing, but resulted in a conference that wasn’t narrowly targeted straight at me anymore, which makes me feel a little sense of loss. In the Old Days it was an affair which made it easy to find people doing more-or-less the same work I was doing, and to compare notes and ideas. I leaned on the conference and IRC channel perhaps a little too heavily, and the changes in that community, plus my working from home since COVID, have made me feel a little more isolated than I used to.

I also acutely feel that I am, to put it mildly, no longer on the ballot for the Smartest Guy in the Room. Like every clichéd engineering-type, I pride myself on my ability to learn things super-fast, grasp new concepts with little to go on, and put things into use quickly. These days, it feels more like I’m running as hard as I can just to stay in place. Some of it is that I have a life at home that needs more attention than it once did, but mostly it’s simply that I’ve lost a few steps and things are harder for me than they used to be.

The burden of non-greatness

This can on occasion fill me with despair.

Over the years, as I’ve started to struggle more it’s become clear that essentially my whole sense of self is based around the ideal, "Don’t be a burden." Once I managed to articulate this particular psychosis to myself, it was easy to see in my dad and uncles, but of course recognizing it isn’t the same as successfully dealing with it.

Yes, that’s simplistic and reeking of traditional gender roles. No, it’s not healthy. Yes, I’m from Minnesota and grew up German-Catholic, why do you ask?

Part of working in a technical field is that it’s relatively easy to tell how good of a job you — or a colleague — is doing. I’m slowly becoming more of a burden at work —  the guy who you’re not quite sure is going to get his shit done, who can’t be trusted to do the hard stuff anymore, who may need a babysitter of some kind. I know if I were my manager, I’d be considering a lot of those issues when I look at me. There are other reasons for for my decline that I’ll explore in another post, maybe, but it’s unambiguously true that I’m not as productive as I once was, and slower to figure out what’s going on.

Don’t get me wrong — I’m still useful. Just not as much as I used to be. And the idea that people may have to "manage" me (in the "keep an eye on Bill" sense) is disquieting.

I’m not sure exactly what mid-life crisis this puts me in right now. Probably right around 3 or 4. First was when I finally gave up on my dissertation, although calling that "mid-life" is a bit of a stretch. Second was realizing that, despite my expectations, I didn’t like having little kids, and it took me a long time (both for me to "work on myself" and for them to get older) before that sorted itself out. Then there was a bit when I realized I was never going to be a particularly Big Deal, but I don’t know if that one counts. And then there’s now.

Raging against the machine.

It’s AI, of course.

Quick anecdote: Some years back, I was going crazy trying to figure out how to get a performant SQL query that touched a ton of tables. I had foreign keys up the wazoo. I had query hints. I had eventually succumbed to de-normalizing some of the data in a pre-processing step. I don’t doubt that someone could have made it work, but I couldn’t. And then I finally, finally realized: the machine I building for had like 32GB of RAM — which at the time was ungodly. I could do a dumb-as-rocks initial query, load the result into a goddamn perl hash, and do the rest of it in memory. I lost an enormous amount of time trying to solve a problem based on working around constraints that were no longer valid.

Note

The playing field is not just changing, it may well now be alien.

I feel the same way now when working with LLMs. The constraints have changed dramtically. The AI doesn’t get confused with long methods, or 12-branch if/then statements. Writing for a human audience is demonstrably more expensive (at least initially) than writing for agents. Developing expertise in a platform (programming language, hardware, etc.) matters little when the models are so very, very good at porting stuff. "Ergonomics" might mean wildly different things depending on the tooling you’re using and how smart it is. There will come a point when it no longer makes sense to optimize the code "I" produce for human-readability, and I suspect that point may have already passed.

The playing field is not just changing, it may well now be alien.

Getting back on the horse. Or maybe on the pelican on a bike.

This isn’t a "boo hoo, I’m so stupid and old" post. Or, at least not just that. I am finding it easier to apply the stuff I’m actually good at — architecture, algorithms, a hard-won intuition about the underlying data and where it might give us problems — and the LLMs can largely take care of the stuff I’m bad at.

Problem is, the very latest frontier-level models are better than I am at roughly…​everything. I’m not sure how to feel about that yet. I derive an unhealthy about of my self-worth from being smart and useful at work, and as opportunities to feel that way grow more scarce, I’m whistling in the dark and trying to find new ways to motivate myself.

So whatever mid-life (well, "mid" is likely a bit optimisitc) crisis I’m in, I’ve decided to try and work through it by documenting it here. Because until I write it down, I haven’t really thought it through.
