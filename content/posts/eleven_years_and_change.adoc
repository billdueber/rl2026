## Eleven Years...

It's been more than 11 years since I wrote a blog post.

That seems impossibly long ago now. The kids were 7, 5, and 2, and just
trying to get to the end of the day felt like a struggle a lot of the time. I
was going through a mid-life crisis -- no red
 sports car, which I don't know what I would have done with, anyway, but a lot
  of
 self-doubt and
 anxiety about whether I had completely lost any agency in my own life.

Young kids can do that to you, I guess.


My day-to-day work life was built around trying to improve the library catalog
results and, even more so, trying to prove to myself (and thus hopefully others)
that the stuff I was producing was, you know, good. Impressive, even.

I saw blogging as a mechanism to force myself to write, building on an
ingrained belief that writing is a critical component of thinking in general --
that ideas, when expressed in print, emerge more fully formed and solid than
beforehand. Writing, I've tried to convey to my kids, is the act of
trying to convince
yourself that you know what you're talking about. If you haven't written it
down, you haven't really thought it through.

Then Google Reader went dark, and I stopped writing. Apparently, all that lip service to critical thinking and high-minded exploration of ideas was completely subsumed by ego and the desire to be read.

I don't feel too bad about it. A lot of us stopped around the same time.
Dancing like no one is watching is one thing, but writing when no one is reading is clearly another. I'm married to a writer, so I have a little insight into it.

I didn't totally abandon the blog, of course. I just stopped writing in it. I
 changed the technology it was based on more than once, in lieu of actually
 writing, and managed to lose the comments and a lot of the formatting and
 such along the way. None of the old stuff is particularly interesting,
 anyway, and the posts with a more  technical lean are so hilariously out of
 date that they're more "fun" than useful.

I've been having a pretty good time going over the old posts.
Part of it is the glimpses of a Me, back then, who's living a remarkably
similar life to the one I'm living now, especially my work life.
Still working at
the University of Michigan Libraries, still focused on programming and
trying to make search better. Many of the technologies have changed,
as have the types of data I'm dealing with on the regular, but it's nothing I
wouldn't
have recognized ten years ago. Libraries aren't particularly dynamic,
no matter what we tell ourselves.

The tone on those old posts is a little...strident, maybe? I had clearly heard someone use the phrase,
"Personal Brand" at some point and taken it to heart. But it's mostly good or good-enough work, and I'm still
the best audience for my own humor, so that's all a lot of fun.

The point being, I guess, that I'm actually in a position to look back a
decade or more
and make a valid, apples-to-apple-like-fruit comparison.

A big part of what happened over the last ten years is that, in a lot of ways, I "settled in" to the job. I
acquired all that vaunted "Organizational knowledge" that I was so quick to mock in the past. I got more
familiar with the data we deal with, and the methods we use. I got to know more people at more institutions,
and then watch most of them move on as they got older, switching careers or moving into management. I like to
think that I didn't get complacent, but I did get, for lack of a better word, "comfortable."

My hair got gray, my forehead turned into a "five-head" ("Like a forehead,
but One Bigger"), and some recent
retirements turned me into the Old Man of my group. Given that I have a
14-year-old kid, retirement is not going to be an
option anytime soon, so I'll be soldiering on despite being 57 now. Things
haven't always been smooth, but they were always at least a little familiar.

## ...and Change

I didn't fool myself into thinking things were static, of course. My
technical expertise fell out of date, colleagues started getting younger and
smarter, all of which made me think fondly (and/or bitterly, depending on the
day) of when I was able to consider
myself Young and Smart.

Code4Lib turned into a conference that was more welcoming to more interests and skill levels, which is an
unambiguously Good Thing, but resulted in a conference that wasn't
narrowly targeted straight at me anymore, which
makes me feel a little sense of loss. It was an avenue which made it easy to
find people doing more-or-less the same work I was doing, and to compare
notes and ideas. I leaned on the conference and IRC channel perhaps a little
too heavily, and the changes there plus my working from home since COVID
have made me feel a little more isolated than I used to.

I also acutely feel that I am, to put it mildly, no longer on the ballot for the Smartest Guy in the Room.
Like every clichéd engineering-types, I pride myself on my ability to learn
things super-fast, grasp new
concepts with little to go on, and put things into use quickly. These days, it feels more like I'm running as
hard as I can just to stay in place. Some of it is that I have a life at home
that needs more attention than it
once did, but mostly I've lost a few steps and things are
just harder for me.

## The burden of non-greatness

This can on occasion fill me with despair.

Over the years, as I've started to struggle more it's become clear that
essentially my whole sense of self is based around the
ideal, "Don't be a burden."

Yes, that's simplistic and reeking of traditional gender roles. No, it's not
healthy.

Once I
managed to articulate it to myself, it was easy to see in my dad and uncles,
but of course recognizing it isn't the same
as successfully dealing with it.

Part of working in a technical field is that it's relatively
easy to tell how good of a job you -- or a colleague -- is doing.
I'm slowly becoming more of a burden at work --  the guy
who you're not quite sure is
going to get his shit done, who can't be trusted to do the hard stuff
anymore, who may need a babysitter of some kind. I know if I were my manager,
I'd be considering a lot of those issues when I look at me. There are other
reasons for that which I'll explore in another post, maybe.

Don't get me wrong -- I'm still useful. Just not as much as I used to be,
and the idea that people have to "manage" me (in the "keeping an eye on Bill"
 sense) is disquieting.

I'm not sure exactly what mid-life crisis this puts me in right now. Probably
right around 3 or 4. First was when I finally gave up on my dissertation.
Second was realizing that despite my expectations, I didn't like having
little kids. Then there was a bit when I realized I was never going to be
a particularly Big Deal, but I don't know if that one counts. And then
there's now.

## Getting back on the horse. Or maybe on the pelican on a bike.

This isn't a "boo hoo, I'm so stupid and old" post. Or, at least not _just_ that. Because the whole LLM
revolution has really kind of kick-started me again. I'm finding it easier to apply the stuff I'm actually
good at -- architecture, algorithms, a hard-won intuition about the underlying data and where it might give us
problems -- and the LLMs can largely take care of the stuff I'm bad at.

So, for the foreseeable future, I'm going to make a real effort to understand
what it
 all
means for me, in a very narrow, narcissistic way. I'm not sure I'm willing to
 call it
a "revolution" as of yet, but I'm damn close. It'll depend on how much it
actually costs once the dust settles -- we really don't know how much it's
costing the private US companies to build and serve their models yet --
but I'm really enjoying myself in the meantime.

So whatever mid-life (two-thirds life?) crisis I'm in,
I'm going to do something new and document it here. Because
until I write it down, I haven't really thought it through.
